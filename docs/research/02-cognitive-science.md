# Cognitive Science and Developmental Psychology of Learning, Ages 5–9

**Purpose of this document.** Evidence base for a home learning system covering the last kindergarten year and the first three school years (~ages 5–9), delivered in English for an Estonian family. Downstream deliverables: printable exercise booklets (first: picture sudoku) and a Civilization-style skill tree running from age-5 foundations to eight target careers. Every section ends with "design implications"; the whole document ends with a consolidated table of exercise-design rules.

**Context notes specific to this project.**

- In Estonia formal school starts at age 7, so "last kindergarten year" here means the *koolieelik* year (6–7) plus possibly the year before (5–6). The evidence below is organized by age, not by institution.
- The child is likely bilingual-in-progress (Estonian home/kindergarten, English materials). Estonian has one of the most transparent orthographies in Europe; English has the least transparent (Seymour, Aro & Erskine, 2003). This asymmetry matters enormously for the reading section (§10) and is flagged there.

## 1. Piaget: the classical stage account and what survives of it

### 1.1 The classical claims

Jean Piaget (*The Psychology of the Child*, 1966, with Bärbel Inhelder) placed ages 5–9 across two stages:

| Stage | Piaget's ages | Signature behaviors |
|---|---|---|
| Preoperational | ~2–7 | Symbolic play, egocentrism (three-mountains task), centration (attending to one dimension), failure of conservation (a taller glass "has more juice"), no reversibility |
| Concrete operational | ~7–11 | Conservation of number → length → mass → volume (in that rough order, "horizontal décalage"), class inclusion, seriation, transitivity — but only on **concrete, manipulable** content, not abstract propositions |

So the 5–9 window straddles Piaget's single biggest developmental transition: the arrival of reversible mental operations around age 6–7.

### 1.2 Modern revisions — what to keep, what to drop

The modern consensus (usefully summarized in any recent edition of Siegler, *How Children Develop*) revises Piaget in five ways:

1. **Competence was underestimated.** With simplified, meaningful tasks children succeed years earlier. Rochel **Gelman** showed number conservation in 3–4-year-olds with small sets; Margaret **Donaldson** (*Children's Minds*, 1978) showed that "human sense" versions of tasks (a "naughty teddy" messing up the row instead of a solemn adult rearranging it) dissolve much apparent egocentrism. **Lesson: task framing and set size, not raw logic, are often the bottleneck.**
2. **Development is domain-specific, not one great stage.** A child can be "concrete operational" in number and preoperational in another domain. Skill trees should therefore be per-domain, not per-age.
3. **Change is gradual and variable, not stagewise.** Robert **Siegler's** "overlapping waves" model (*Emerging Minds*, 1996): at any moment a child holds *multiple* strategies (e.g., for 5+3: count-all, count-on from the larger, retrieval) and shifts the frequency distribution among them over years. Regression is normal; the same child may count-on today and count-all tomorrow when tired.
4. **Neo-Piagetian reframing: stages are working-memory limits.** Juan **Pascual-Leone** and Robbie **Case** re-derived stage-like behavior from growth in mental capacity ("M-space"). Conservation "arrives" when the child can hold both height and width in mind at once. This converges with the executive-function literature (§3) and is the most actionable revision.
5. **What survives:** the broad ordering (concrete before abstract), the reality of the 5–7 shift, the value of self-driven exploration, and horizontal décalage (mastery in one material does not transfer automatically to another — practice conservation-type reasoning across many materials).

**Design implications.** (a) Ages 5–6: keep everything concrete, countable, small-set, and framed as a story a child recognizes. (b) Do not gate content by chronological age; gate by demonstrated prerequisite skills (this is precisely what a skill tree does well). (c) Expect and design for strategy variability — the same exercise type should reappear across months. (d) Abstract/symbolic versions of a task come *after* the concrete version is easy, never instead of it.

## 2. Vygotsky: ZPD, scaffolding, and the social layer

Lev **Vygotsky** (*Mind in Society*, published 1978) contributed three load-bearing ideas:

1. **Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD):** the band between what a child can do alone and what they can do with help. Learning happens in that band; tasks below it bore, tasks above it overwhelm. Crucially, the ZPD is *assessed dynamically* — by trying a slightly harder item with a hint, not by a static test.
2. **Scaffolding** — the term is actually from **Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976)**, "The role of tutoring in problem solving" (*Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry*), studying 3–5-year-olds building pyramids. Their six scaffolding functions are a ready-made checklist: recruit interest, reduce degrees of freedom, maintain direction, mark critical features, control frustration, demonstrate. The defining property of good scaffolding is **contingent fading**: help is withdrawn as competence grows ("transfer of responsibility").
3. **Private speech.** Children aged ~4–8 talk themselves through hard tasks; this out-loud self-regulation later internalizes into verbal thought. It is a feature, not a distraction — and it predicts better task performance on hard-but-doable problems.

**Design implications.** (a) Every exercise family needs a **difficulty ladder with built-in fading**: version 1 with a worked row / heavy visual support → version N bare. A printable booklet can fade scaffolds page by page. (b) A parent-facing "if stuck, say this" hint line at the bottom of hard pages operationalizes contingent tutoring for a non-teacher parent. (c) Picture sudoku is a textbook ZPD ladder: 4×4 with 3 blanks → 4×4 with 8 blanks → 6×6 → 9×9 with symbols → 9×9 with digits. (d) Encourage thinking aloud explicitly ("It helps to say your thinking out loud").

## 3. Executive function and working memory, 5–9

### 3.1 The three core executive functions

Adele **Diamond's** canonical review ("Executive Functions," *Annual Review of Psychology*, 2013) identifies three separable but correlated core EFs, all developing steeply across 5–9:

| EF | What it is | Age 5–9 status | Classic child measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inhibitory control | Suppressing a prepotent response | Big gains 3–6, continued gains to adolescence; 5-year-olds fail Stroop-like conflicts that 8-year-olds pass | Day/Night task, Head-Toes-Knees-Shoulders |
| Working memory | Holding *and manipulating* information | Capacity roughly doubles between 4 and 11 | Backward digit span; Corsi blocks |
| Cognitive flexibility | Switching rules/perspectives | Latest to mature; rule-switching costs still large at 7–9 | Dimensional Change Card Sort (children pass basic version ~4–5, harder versions much later) |

EF at school entry predicts academic outcomes as strongly as IQ does — early math and attention skills at school entry were the strongest predictors of later achievement in **Duncan et al. (2007)**, "School readiness and later achievement" (*Developmental Psychology*, six longitudinal datasets).

### 3.2 Working-memory numbers you can design against

**Gathercole, Pickering, Ambridge & Wearing (2004)** and Gathercole & Alloway's practitioner work (*Working Memory and Learning*, 2008) give usable norms:

- Forward digit span: ~4 items at age 5, ~5 at age 7, ~5–6 at age 9 (adult ~7).
- **Nelson Cowan's** deeper estimate: the true attention-limited capacity is ~3–4 chunks in adults and *fewer* in children — assume **2–3 independent chunks** for a 5-year-old on a novel task.
- WM is the resource that runs out first in multi-step instructions. Gathercole's classroom observations: children who "won't follow instructions" are usually children whose WM was exceeded ("put your sheets on the green table, arrow cards in the packet, pencil away and come sit on the carpet" = 4-step instruction = fail at 5–6).
- Chunking and automaticity are the escape hatch: once counting-on or letter–sound mapping is automatic, it costs ~0 WM and frees capacity for the actual problem.

### 3.3 Can EF be trained?

Honest answer: **direct EF training transfers poorly.** Computerized WM training (CogMed etc.) shows near-transfer only (Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013 meta-analysis). Curricular approaches (Tools of the Mind — Bodrova & Leong; Diamond et al., *Science*, 2007) show promising but inconsistently replicated effects. Diamond's own conclusion: EFs improve most as a *by-product* of engaging, gradually-harder real activities the child cares about (games with rules, music, sports, pretend play with roles) — not from drilling EF itself.

**Design implications.** (a) **Never exceed ~3 things to hold in mind** in an instruction or a single reasoning step for ages 5–6; ~4 by 8–9. (b) Instructions: one line, one verb, an example row instead of prose. (c) Externalize memory: the puzzle sheet itself should hold the state (tick boxes, symbol banks printed at the top, scratch space) so the head doesn't have to. (d) Games-with-rules (sudoku included) *are* the age-appropriate EF workout — inhibiting "the symbol I want to write" in favor of "the symbol that must go here" is inhibition + WM training in disguise. (e) Automate the bottom of the skill tree (counting, letter sounds, number bonds to 10) precisely *because* automaticity frees WM for everything above it.

## 4. Cognitive load theory

John **Sweller** (1988; Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, 1998/2019 reviews) starts from the same WM bottleneck and derives instructional rules. Load comes in three flavors: **intrinsic** (element interactivity of the material itself, relative to expertise), **extraneous** (imposed by bad presentation), **germane** (useful schema-building work). Effects with strong empirical support and direct relevance:

| Effect | Finding | Booklet translation |
|---|---|---|
| **Worked-example effect** | Novices learn more from studying worked examples than solving equivalent problems (Sweller & Cooper, 1985) | Start each new exercise type with one fully solved example, then **completion problems** (partly solved), then full problems — the "fading" of Renkl & Atkinson |
| **Split-attention effect** | Separating text from the diagram it explains forces costly mental integration | Put instructions *inside/next to* the puzzle grid; label the picture, don't write a paragraph above it |
| **Redundancy effect** | Adding text that repeats what the picture already says *hurts* | If the example row shows the rule, don't also explain it in words a 5-year-old can't read anyway |
| **Expertise-reversal effect** (Kalyuga) | Scaffolds that help novices *hinder* the competent | The booklet ladder must actually remove worked examples and hints in later sets — do not keep training wheels on |
| **Element interactivity** | Difficulty = number of elements that must be processed *simultaneously* | Grade puzzle difficulty by interacting elements, not by superficial size: a 4×4 sudoku needing a two-step inference is harder than a 6×6 needing only single-step scans |
| **Goal-free / low option count** | Reducing simultaneous choices lowers load | Early pages: fewer candidate symbols, fewer blank cells |

**Design implications.** Example → completion → full-problem sequence for every new type; instructions as pictures; ruthlessly delete decorative clutter near the working area (decoration lives on covers and reward pages, not inside puzzles); grade difficulty by inference depth.

## 5. Retrieval practice (the testing effect)

**Roediger & Karpicke (2006, *Psychological Science*)**: actively recalling information strengthens memory far more than restudying it, especially at delays — one of the most replicated effects in learning science (Adesope et al., 2017 meta-analysis, g ≈ 0.5–0.6 vs restudy). Key facts for this age band:

- It works in elementary school children, including K–1, when retrieval is **supported** (cued recall, picture-cued recall) rather than free recall. **Fazio & Marsh (2019, "Retrieval-based learning in children")** review the developmental picture: young children benefit, but need higher initial success and more support than adults.
- **Feedback is essential** for children: retrieval attempt → immediate confirmation of the right answer. Errors followed by correction are not harmful; unstudied wrong guesses followed by feedback can even help ("productive failure" / pretesting effects — but with feedback).
- Low stakes or no stakes: the benefit is cognitive, not evaluative. Quizzing that feels like testing-with-consequences imports anxiety and kills the motivational side (§9).
- Format: flashcards, "cover-and-write," brain dumps ("draw everything you remember about…"), and — for this project — *puzzle types that force recall of previously learned facts* (e.g., a crossnumber requiring number bonds).

**Design implications.** (a) Each booklet opens with a 2-minute "warm-up from last time" retrieval page. (b) Answer keys placed for immediate self-check (fold-over flap or last page). (c) Aim for ~70–90% retrieval success in kids' materials; if the child is failing half the recall items, drop down the ladder. (d) In the skill tree, "unlocked" nodes are not "done" nodes — old nodes keep appearing in warm-ups.

## 6. Spaced repetition

The spacing effect (Ebbinghaus, 1885; ~900 studies since) says distributed practice beats massed practice for retention. Modern anchors:

- **Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008, *Psychological Science*)**: optimal gap between study sessions scales with the retention interval — roughly **gap ≈ 10–20% of the time you want to remember it for**. To remember for a year: revisit at ~1 day, ~1 week, ~3–4 weeks, ~2–3 months.
- Works in children: **Sobel, Cepeda & Kapler (2011)** found 5th-graders retained ~3× more vocabulary with a 1-week gap vs massed; similar results in younger children for math facts and science (Vlach & Sandhofer, 2012, showed spacing benefits in preschool science category learning).
- Expanding schedules (1d → 3d → 1w → 3w) are convenient and roughly as good as uniform spacing; the crime is only ever massing.
- Sleep between sessions is part of the mechanism; two short sessions on different days beat one long session, minute for minute.

**Design implications.** (a) Booklets are the natural spacing unit: booklet N's warm-ups retrieve booklet N−1 (≈1 week), one page retrieves N−4 (≈1 month). (b) Sessions short and frequent: **10–20 minutes/day beats 70 minutes on Sunday** at these ages. (c) The skill tree needs a "maintenance" mechanic (Civ analogy: unlocked techs generate a small upkeep — a node re-tested quarterly stays lit; long-neglected nodes visually "dim" and get pulled into warm-ups).

## 7. Interleaving

**Rohrer & Taylor (2007)** and especially **Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic (2015, *JEP*)** with 7th-grade math: interleaved practice (mixing problem *types* within a session) massively outperformed blocked practice at a 30-day delayed test (d ≈ 1.0+ in the 2015 study), even though blocked practice *feels* better and produces better same-day performance. Mechanism: interleaving forces the child to **choose the strategy**, not just execute it, and highlights discriminative contrasts between confusable categories (Kornell & Bjork, 2008, "inductive learning" with paintings).

Caveats that matter for 5–9:

- **Blocked first, then interleave.** Novices need a short blocked phase to acquire the procedure at all; interleaving pays off once each component skill exists. (This is the interleaving–CLT truce.)
- Interleave **confusable** things (addition vs subtraction word problems; b/d/p letter forms; area vs perimeter later) — interleaving unrelated things is just noise.
- Expect and pre-accept the "it feels harder" signal — performance during practice drops while learning rises ("desirable difficulties," **Robert Bjork**).

**Design implications.** (a) Booklet structure: new skill gets one blocked page, then appears *mixed* with older skills for the rest of the booklet and all later ones. (b) Deliberately build mixed pages of confusables: 5+3 next to 5−3; "which is more" next to "how many more". (c) Tell the parent (in a small footer note) that the mixed pages are supposed to feel harder and slower — that's the point — so they don't "helpfully" re-sort them.

## 8. Mastery learning (Bloom)

Benjamin **Bloom**, "Learning for Mastery" (1968): hold *achievement* constant and let *time* vary, instead of the reverse. Cycle: instruction → formative check against an explicit criterion (~80–90%) → targeted correctives for whoever isn't there yet → re-check → only then move on. Bloom's "**2 sigma problem**" (1984) claimed 1:1 tutoring + mastery = 2 SD improvement; the 2-sigma number has not survived meta-analytic scrutiny (Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns, 1990 put mastery learning at ~0.5 SD; modern tutoring meta-analyses like Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan, 2020 find ~0.35 SD for real tutoring programs). **The honest claim: mastery learning is a solidly positive, moderate-effect structure, and it is *the* correct structure for a prerequisite-heavy domain like early math** — because in cumulative subjects, unmastered prerequisites compound (you cannot do multi-digit addition on shaky number bonds).

A skill tree **is** mastery learning made visible: explicit prerequisite edges, explicit unlock criteria, no social comparison (the child races the tree, not other children).

**Design implications.** (a) Every skill-tree node gets a written mastery criterion ("adds within 10 in ≤3 s per fact, 9/10 correct, two sessions apart" — the two-sessions-apart clause bakes in spacing). (b) Failing a check triggers a *different* corrective exercise, not the same page again. (c) Progression is by mastery, never by age or calendar. (d) Keep checks tiny and framed as "unlock trials," not tests.

## 9. Motivation: self-determination theory, praise, and growth mindset (with caveats)

### 9.1 Self-determination theory (SDT)

**Deci & Ryan** (2000, "Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation"): intrinsic motivation runs on three needs — **autonomy** (I chose this), **competence** (I can feel myself getting better), **relatedness** (people I love are involved and warm about it). Robustly supported across ages and cultures.

The dangerous corollary: **extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation.** Deci (1971); **Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973)** — the classic *overjustification* study where preschoolers rewarded for drawing (an activity they already loved) subsequently drew less. Deci, Koestner & Ryan's (1999) meta-analysis: *expected, tangible, task-contingent* rewards undermine; *unexpected* rewards and *verbal/informational* feedback do not. So: gamification that pays children for doing what could become intrinsically loved is playing with fire; gamification that *marks competence growth* (levels, unlocked abilities, visible tree progress) aligns with SDT.

### 9.2 Praise

**Mueller & Dweck (1998, *JPSP*)**: fifth-graders praised for intelligence ("you're so smart") subsequently chose easier tasks, enjoyed less, performed worse after failure, and lied about scores more than children praised for effort/process. **Gunderson et al. (2013, *Child Development*)**: parents' proportion of *process praise* to 1–3-year-olds predicted the child's incremental beliefs five years later. Practical refinement: praise the **process that actually worked** ("you tried three spots before finding where the frog goes — that checking is what solved it"), not effort in the abstract; praising effort on a failed easy task reads as condescension even to 8-year-olds. Best of all is often **information, not evaluation**: describe what the child did.

### 9.3 Growth mindset — the evidence *and* the caveats

Claim (**Dweck**, *Mindset*, 2006): believing ability is malleable → better challenge-seeking and resilience. Current evidence honestly summarized:

- **Sisk, Burgoyne et al. (2018, *Psychological Science*)** meta-analyses: mindset–achievement correlation is weak overall (r ≈ 0.10), and mindset *interventions* average d ≈ 0.08.
- **Yeager et al. (2019, *Nature*)** — the U.S. National Study, preregistered, n≈12,000: a cheap 1-hour online intervention raised grades of *low-achieving* 9th-graders by ~0.1 GPA points; effects heterogeneous, bigger where peer norms supported challenge-seeking. **Li & Bates** failed to replicate key child findings; a 2023–24 exchange of metas (Macnamara & Burgoyne vs Yeager & Dweck) remains unresolved.
- Sensible read for this project: growth mindset is **not** a magic lever, and mindset *posters/slogans* do nothing. What holds up is the narrower, older praise literature (§9.2), plus designing the environment so that struggle is normal and improvement is visible — which you get from mastery + retrieval design anyway. A skill tree that shows "you couldn't do this in March; you can now" *is* the honest, environmental version of a mindset intervention.

### 9.4 Age-specific motivation facts

Children aged 5–7 are natural optimists about their own ability (Stipek's work: nearly all kindergarteners think they're near the top of the class); ability self-assessments become accurate — and motivation becomes fragile — around 8–10. The system's motivational job is mostly *not breaking* the optimism: no percentile comparisons, no red ink, failure framed as "not yet unlocked."

**Design implications.** (a) Choice within structure: child picks *which* of 2–3 unlocked exercises today (autonomy) but the tree gates the options (mastery). (b) Rewards mark competence, never bribe activity: unlock animations/stickers for *new abilities*, not payment-per-page. (c) Script the parent's praise on the booklet footer ("say what strategy you saw them use"). (d) Language throughout: "not yet," "level up," "training," never "smart," never scores-vs-siblings. (e) Relatedness: design some pages explicitly as parent–child pair activities (parent has a role card).

## 10. Science of reading: what to do about literacy

### 10.1 The settled core

- **Simple View of Reading** (Gough & Tunmer, 1986): Reading comprehension = Decoding × Language comprehension. Both factors are necessary; neither is sufficient.
- **Scarborough's Reading Rope** (2001) unpacks the two strands: word recognition (phonological awareness, decoding, sight recognition) becoming automatic, woven with language comprehension (vocabulary, background knowledge, syntax, verbal reasoning) becoming strategic.
- **Systematic, explicit phonics works.** National Reading Panel (2000); UK Rose Review (2006); **Castles, Rastle & Nation (2018)**, "Ending the Reading Wars" (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) — the definitive modern review. Sequence: phonemic awareness (blending/segmenting spoken sounds) → grapheme–phoneme correspondences taught in a planned order → blending for reading, segmenting for spelling → decodable practice texts early on → orthographic mapping turns decoded words into instantly recognized ones (**Linnea Ehri's** phase theory: pre-alphabetic → partial → full → consolidated alphabetic).
- Three-cueing / "look at the picture and guess" strategies are discredited as a *word-reading* method; comprehension strategies and rich read-alouds remain valuable for the language strand.

### 10.2 The Estonian angle (this actually changes the plan)

**Seymour, Aro & Erskine (2003, *British Journal of Psychology*)** compared 13 European orthographies: children learning transparent orthographies (Finnish, Estonian-like) reach ~90%+ accurate decoding within about one year of instruction; English-learning children take **2.5+ years** to reach comparable accuracy because English GPCs are inconsistent. Consequences:

- The child will likely learn to *decode Estonian* fast and mostly at school/kindergarten. Do not fight or duplicate that; it's an asset (phonemic awareness transfers across languages).
- English materials must **not assume English decoding ability at age 5–7.** Early booklets should be doable from pictures + minimal sight words, with any English text meant for the *parent to read aloud*.
- When teaching English reading explicitly (sensible from ~6–7, after Estonian decoding is underway), use a **systematic synthetic phonics sequence** (e.g., the SATPIN-style ordering: s,a,t,p,i,n first — allows many words immediately), expect interference points (Estonian letters map 1:1; English 'a' maps many ways), and teach English GPCs as "English's tricky code" explicitly.
- Meanwhile feed the *language comprehension* strand in English hard from age 5: read-alouds, audio, vocabulary-rich content — this strand has no transparency discount and takes years.

**Design implications.** (a) Booklets for 5–6: picture-first, near-zero required reading; instruction lines written to the parent. (b) An explicit English-phonics sub-tree starting ~age 6–7 with a planned GPC order and decodable mini-texts; separate node track for "English vocabulary & listening" starting at 5. (c) Phonemic-awareness games (oral, no print: "what's the first sound in *frog*?") as the true root node of all literacy. (d) Handwriting/letter formation practiced in whichever script order the school uses; b/d/p discrimination pages use interleaving (§7).

## 11. Early number sense

### 11.1 What's in the head before school

- **Approximate Number System (ANS):** infants and all humans discriminate quantities ratio-dependently (**Feigenson, Dehaene & Spelke, 2004**; Halberda et al., 2008 linked ANS acuity to math achievement). It gives "more/less" intuition but not exact arithmetic.
- **Subitizing:** instant exact recognition of 1–3 (later 4–5, and structured patterns like dice-6) without counting. Perceptual subitizing → conceptual subitizing ("I see 4 and 3, that's 7") is a major early lever (Doug Clements' work).
- **Counting principles** (Gelman & Gallistel, 1978): one-one correspondence, stable order, **cardinality** (the last word said *is* the set size — the big age-3.5–4.5 conceptual leap), abstraction, order-irrelevance. Cardinality must be secure before arithmetic means anything.

### 11.2 The 5–9 development that matters

- **Number-line representation shifts from logarithmic to linear.** Siegler & **Booth** (2004): K children place numbers on a 0–100 line logarithmically (small numbers spread out, big compressed); by grade 2 it's linear for 0–100; the same log→linear shift replays for 0–1000 in grades 2–4. **Linearity of number-line estimates predicts arithmetic and overall math achievement.**
- **Siegler & Ramani (2008/2009):** playing a simple *linear* numbered 1–10 board game (à la Chutes & Ladders, moving and naming squares) for ~1 hour total across sessions substantially improved low-income preschoolers' number-line estimation, magnitude comparison, counting and numeral ID — and *circular* boards didn't. Cheap, printable, directly copyable.
- **Arithmetic strategy development** (Siegler): count-all → count-on from larger → decomposition (7+6 = 7+3+3) → retrieval. Fluent retrieval of bonds to 10/20 by ~age 7–8 is the automaticity that unlocks multi-digit work (§3.2).
- **Concrete → Representational → Abstract (CRA):** objects → pictures/ten-frames/number lines → symbols; strong support in early-math instruction research. Ten-frames and rekenrek-style images build "5 and 10 structure."
- Early math at school entry is the *strongest* single predictor of later achievement — stronger than early reading — in **Duncan et al. (2007)**.
- **Finger use is fine** at 5–7 (fingers are a natural embodied ten-frame; banning them early is counterproductive); it fades naturally as retrieval takes over.
- Watch-outs: teach the **equals sign as "same value both sides"** early (huge later-algebra dividend, per Nicole McNeil's work), vary problem formats (8 = 5 + _), and include zero and "how many more" comparison problems, which children find hardest.

**Design implications.** (a) Root math nodes: subitizing dot-flash pages, counting collections, cardinality games — before any written arithmetic. (b) Print the **linear number-path board game** as an early deliverable (evidence-backed, nearly free). (c) Ten-frame representation used consistently across booklets as the house style for quantity. (d) Number-line estimation pages ("mark where 37 lives") from age 6 up, expanding range with age. (e) Timed-but-gentle retrieval ladders for bonds to 10 → 20 → tables, only after strategies are understood (mastery gates, §8). (f) Picture sudoku slots in as the *non-numeric logic* branch: it exercises deduction, inhibition and WM with zero arithmetic prerequisite — a perfect age-5 entry node that later feeds toward "systematic search" and "proof-like reasoning" nodes on the software-engineer/doctor branches.

## 12. Consolidated table: exercise-design rules derived from the evidence

| # | Rule | Evidence base | Concrete application in this project |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gate by prerequisite mastery, never by age or calendar | Bloom mastery; Piaget-revised domain-specificity; Duncan 2007 | Skill tree edges = prerequisites; each node has an explicit unlock criterion (e.g., "9/10 correct, twice, ≥3 days apart") |
| 2 | One instruction line, ≤3 things to hold in mind (age 5–6), ≤4 (8–9) | Cowan/Gathercole WM norms | Instructions as a solved example row, not prose; parent-facing text separated at page bottom |
| 3 | Every new exercise type: worked example → completion (partly solved) → full problems | Worked-example & completion effects (Sweller; Renkl) | Page 1 of any new type shows a solved puzzle; pages 2–3 half-solved; then bare |
| 4 | Fade scaffolds explicitly across pages and booklets | Wood/Bruner/Ross scaffolding; expertise reversal (Kalyuga) | Picture sudoku: symbol bank printed → symbol bank smaller → none; hints shrink to nothing by set's end |
| 5 | Keep the working area visually clean; integrate text into graphics; no decorative clutter near the task | Split-attention & redundancy effects | Decoration on covers and reward pages only; labels inside the grid |
| 6 | Grade difficulty by inference depth / interacting elements, not by surface size | Element interactivity (CLT) | Sudoku levels defined by required deduction chain length (scan → single elimination → two-step), not just grid size |
| 7 | Sessions: 10–20 min/day, several days a week; never one long weekly block | Spacing effect; Cepeda 2008 | Booklet paced as daily 2-page sittings; parent guide states this |
| 8 | Start every session with 2 minutes of retrieval of older material, with immediate answer check | Testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke); feedback necessity in children (Fazio & Marsh) | "Warm-up from last time" page opening each booklet section; fold-over answer flap |
| 9 | Revisit mastered skills at expanding gaps (~1 day, 1 week, 1 month, 1 quarter) | Cepeda et al. gap-ratio findings; Sobel 2011 | Booklet N warm-ups sample booklets N−1 and N−4; tree nodes "dim" without quarterly maintenance and re-enter warm-ups |
| 10 | Block briefly when a skill is brand new, then interleave it with confusable older skills forever after | Rohrer 2015; Kornell & Bjork; CLT for novices | One blocked page per new skill; all subsequent pages mixed (5+3 beside 5−3; b beside d) |
| 11 | Target ~70–90% success on practice; if below, drop a ladder rung, don't repeat the same page | Retrieval success needs in children; ZPD; mastery correctives | Each node ships with parallel "corrective" variants using different representations |
| 12 | Externalize memory onto the page | WM limits; Gathercole classroom findings | Scratch boxes, candidate-mark corners in sudoku, ten-frames printed, checklists as pictures |
| 13 | Concrete → pictorial → symbolic, per skill (not per child) | Piaget ordering that survived; CRA research | Quantity always shown as objects/ten-frames before digits; sudoku uses pictures before letters/digits |
| 14 | Small sets and child-sensible story framing at 5–6 | Gelman; Donaldson | Numbers ≤5 then ≤10 at entry; tasks framed with animals/food/play, not abstract prompts |
| 15 | Reward *new competence*, never pay for activity; no scores against others | SDT; overjustification (Lepper 1973; Deci meta-1999); Stipek | Tree unlocks + ability names ("You can now: add in your head!"); no per-page tokens, no sibling comparisons, no percentiles |
| 16 | Offer bounded choice each session | SDT autonomy | Child picks today's 1–2 pages from 2–3 unlocked options |
| 17 | Script process-praise and strategy-noticing for the parent | Mueller & Dweck 1998; Gunderson 2013 | Footer line per hard page: "Notice aloud the strategy they used"; sample sentence provided |
| 18 | Frame failure as "not yet unlocked"; keep struggle normal and visible progress salient | Mindset literature's *defensible core*; Bjork desirable difficulties | Tree shows before/after ("locked in March → yours in May"); mixed pages carry a note that slower = learning |
| 19 | Age 5–7 English materials must not require English decoding | Seymour 2003 orthography-depth findings | Picture-first booklets; English text addressed to the parent to read aloud; oral English vocab track separate |
| 20 | Literacy root = oral phonemic awareness; then systematic synthetic phonics with decodables (English from ~6–7, explicitly contrasted with Estonian's 1:1 code) | NRP 2000; Castles/Rastle/Nation 2018; Ehri phases | Phonemic-awareness game nodes precede all print nodes; planned GPC order (SATPIN-style); "English is a tricky code" framing |
| 21 | Math root = subitizing, counting principles, cardinality; then linear board game and number-line estimation; fact fluency only after strategies | Gelman & Gallistel; Siegler & Ramani 2008; Siegler & Booth 2004 | Dot-flash pages, counting collections, printable 1–10 linear number-path game, "where does 37 live" pages, gated bond-fluency ladders |
| 22 | Teach '=' as balance, vary equation formats, allow fingers early | McNeil; embodied-counting research | 8 = 5 + □ formats from the start; no finger bans in parent guide |
| 23 | Use games-with-rules as the EF workout; don't drill "brain training" | Diamond 2013; Melby-Lervåg & Hulme 2013 | Sudoku, pattern games, Simon-says-style oral games as first-column tree nodes; no abstract WM drills |
| 24 | Same deep skill across many surface materials (expect non-transfer) | Horizontal décalage; overlapping waves | Each reasoning skill appears in ≥3 costumes across booklets (sudoku rule ≈ seating-chart puzzle ≈ colour-grid puzzle) |

## 13. Quick age-band cheat sheet for the booklet pipeline

| Band | WM budget | Reading assumption (English) | Math frontier (typical, gate by mastery) | Session | Sudoku ladder |
